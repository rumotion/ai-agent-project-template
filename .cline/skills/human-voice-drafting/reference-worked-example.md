# Worked example — before and after

The scenario below is generic. It reproduces the shape of a real failure without reproducing its content:
the sender had listed several concerns for a senior colleague without saying what he wanted done with them,
the colleague replied that she could not tell what was being asked, and the sender's follow-up was the
message a third party then identified as AI-written.

**The rule this illustrates: the content was correct and the email still failed.**

---

## Before — the draft that was flagged

> Hi Dana,
>
> That's on me — I sent you five things I was worried about and never said what I wanted you to do with
> them. Let me try again.
>
> What I'm asking for is one thing: **read the draft response and either tell me it's fine to send, or
> change the wording you're not happy with.** I haven't sent it. I won't until you come back.
>
> Why I'm bothering you with it at all: what we send doesn't stay with them. They copy our answers into
> their own filing, so it ends up in front of their reviewers as our description of how the work was done.
> I'd rather it was checked once now.
>
> You're right that they're not all questions for you. Two of them are, and I can't answer those without
> you:
>
> 1. **We reused material from the earlier engagement as reference.** The form says nothing of theirs was
>    used, and their definition of "their material" is very wide — content, data, metadata, IP. So either
>    we drop the reference from our answer, which would be untrue, or that answer is wrong. **Does reused
>    reference material count?** I don't know, and I'd rather we fix it than have their reviewer find it.
>
> 2. **Our response ends by calling it our complete and final account.** I haven't seen the agreement, so I
>    can't tell whether that promises more than we signed up for. You can see it. Your call, not mine.
>
> The other three aren't questions. They're facts, and I'm confident in them. I just want you comfortable
> with how flatly we're saying them, because once it's written down we're stuck with it.
>
> So: two need an answer from you, one needs someone to look at the agreement, and the rest just need a yes
> or a better sentence.
>
> If you'd rather talk it through than write it up, fifteen minutes works for me.
>
> Thanks,
>
> Alex

## What is wrong with it

| Line | Tell |
|---|---|
| "That's on me — I sent you five things… Let me try again." | Confessional opener, balanced parallel clause, em-dash pivot, announced retry. Four tells in two sentences. |
| Em-dashes in four separate places | The most recognisable marker of all. |
| "**read the draft response and either tell me it's fine to send…**" | Bold inside a running sentence. |
| "Why I'm bothering you with it at all:" | Labelled pseudo-header inside prose. |
| Bolded lead sentence on every numbered item | Makes an email look like generated structured output. |
| "So: two need an answer from you, one needs someone to look at the agreement, and the rest just need a yes or a better sentence." | Tricolon summary recapping what the reader has just read. |
| "Your call, not mine." | Performed humility as a catchphrase. |
| "I'd rather it was checked once now" / "I'd rather we fix it than have their reviewer find it" | Same construction twice. |
| Evenly sized paragraphs throughout | Assistant rhythm. |

Every item raised was real and the priority order was right. That is exactly what makes it worth codifying.

---

## After — the same message, rewritten

Roughly 270 words against the original 380. Zero em-dashes, no bold, no summary paragraph.

> Hi Dana,
>
> Sorry, my last email wasn't clear about what I needed from you.
>
> It's one thing: can you read the draft response and either tell me it's fine to send or change the
> wording you don't like. I haven't sent it and I won't until you come back to me.
>
> The reason I'm asking at all is that our answers don't stay with them. They copy them into their own
> filing, so they end up in front of their reviewers as our description of how the work was done. I'd
> rather it was checked now.
>
> You're right that they're not all questions for you. Two of them are, and I can't answer those without
> you.
>
> 1. We reused material from the earlier engagement as reference. The form says nothing of theirs was used,
>    and their definition of "their material" is very wide: content, data, metadata, IP. So either we drop
>    the reference from our answer, which isn't true, or that answer is wrong. Does reused reference
>    material count?
>
> 2. The response ends by calling it our complete and final account. I haven't seen the agreement so I
>    can't tell whether that promises more than we actually signed up for. You can see it and I can't.
>
> The other three are facts and I'm confident in them. I just want you comfortable with how flatly we say
> them, because once it's written down we're stuck with it. I've marked those three in the draft.
>
> Happy to talk it through if that's quicker, fifteen minutes would do it.
>
> Thanks,
>
> Alex

## What changed, and why it reads differently

- **The apology is six words and then it stops.** A person regrets being unclear. They do not describe the
  shape of their own error.
- **Two bolded lists became one numbered list and one ordinary paragraph.** Five bolded items reads as
  generated output. Two numbered questions and a paragraph covering the rest reads as someone thinking.
- **The summary sentence is gone** and nothing is lost. The reader has just read it.
- **"I don't know" replaces "above my pay grade"**, and **"You can see it and I can't" replaces "Your call,
  not mine."** Same deference, said rather than performed.
- **Sentence lengths vary.** One paragraph is a single line. That alone breaks the assistant rhythm more
  than any individual word choice.
